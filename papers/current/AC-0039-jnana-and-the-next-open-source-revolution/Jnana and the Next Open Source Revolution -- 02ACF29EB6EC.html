Abstract

Open source made source code inspectable, modifiable, redistributable, and forkable under declared licenses and social institutions. AI coding agents change a different variable: the volume, speed, and reach of proposed transformation. When generation, dependency choice, shell execution, build mutation, and deployment occur through tools with broad ambient access, source visibility alone does not reveal who authorized an effect, which constraints applied, or whether the transformation can be reconstructed and refused.

Jnana proposes a constitutional transformation substrate for this problem. AI is treated as a proposer rather than an authority. Proposed changes are represented in governed intermediate form, evaluated under versioned law, lowered through admitted transformation machinery, mediated at effect boundaries, and bound to receipts. Refusal, deferment, branch, rollback, and recovery are legitimate outcomes.

This paper presents Jnana as a possible extension of open-source sovereignty, not a replacement for Linux, Git, maintainers, review, testing, licensing, or community governance. It turns “open power” into specific requirements: inspectable authority, bounded capability, reconstructable transformation, portable governance, provider exit, visible exceptions, and negative power. It also states the assurance boundary. An AI cannot execute arbitrary system effects only if every relevant path is isolated or mediated; deterministic replay exists only under a declared environment closure; hardware sovereignty extends only to the verified hardware and firmware boundary.

The paper integrates Jnana, Jnana II, MCIR, Governed Agentic Coding, Governance Coverage Gaps, GMB, and the universal GSM into an incremental adoption program. No implementation, containment result, Linux integration, hardware proof, or publication authority is claimed.

1. The Problem Is Transformation Authority

Code visibility does not by itself answer:

  • who or what proposed a change;
  • which source, dependency, model, tool, and policy versions contributed;
  • who possessed authority to approve it;
  • which capabilities were granted during construction and execution;
  • which effects occurred;
  • what was refused or omitted; and
  • whether the result can be reconstructed without the original provider.

Open-source projects already use review, testing, signed commits, reproducible-build techniques, sandboxing, and community governance to address parts of this problem. The source’s stronger claims should be narrowed: these practices are not universal, and no claim that conventional systems lack all substrate-level governance is established here.

Jnana targets the remaining structural problem: transformation authority is often external to the artifact being transformed and weakly bound to the resulting effect.

2. Bounded Definition of Jnana

Jnana is a proposed constitutional transformation substrate that coordinates:

  • MCIR: a canonical candidate representation carrying semantic and governance commitments;
  • MCIR-Law: versioned admissibility rules over proposals and transformation regimes;
  • constitutional invariants: higher-order limits on ordinary law mutation;
  • PEIR: a source-defined procedural execution representation whose current corpus identity and conformance remain to be adjudicated;
  • Domain Effects: declared classes and membranes for contact with system resources;
  • ReceiptLog: evidence and lineage for decisions, transformations, effects, and revision; and
  • recovery epochs: explicit emergency, rollback, and post-exception review.

The biological language in the Jnana family is metaphorical architecture, not evidence that software is an organism. This paper emphasizes the transformation-substrate interpretation because it connects directly to open-source practice.

3. AI as Proposer Is an Enforced Role

“AI as proposer” is not achieved by naming the model a proposer. It requires an execution architecture in which model output lacks direct standing and cannot bypass the authorization path.

The minimum invariant is:

Model output may describe or propose an effect, but only an independently authorized and mediated realization may produce that effect.

This requires separation among:

  • model inference and effect execution;
  • proposal identity and author/maintainer standing;
  • capability request and capability grant;
  • generated code and admitted artifact;
  • review evidence and decision authority; and
  • execution receipt and semantic truth.

If an agent can write files, run a shell, alter CI, invoke a provider, or modify credentials outside the governed route, the proposer boundary is incomplete. Governance Coverage Gaps supplies the diagnostic for those escape paths.

4. The Constitutional Transformation Path

A bounded transformation can be represented as:

T = (I, P, A, L, D, X, W, V),

where I identifies exact inputs and dependencies, P the proposal, A authority, L applicable law, D the decision, X realized effects, W receipts and witnesses, and V verification and revision state.

The intended path is:

  1. bind exact source, tool, dependency, model, and environment identities;
  2. represent the proposed transformation and its requested capabilities;
  3. resolve authority and applicable law;
  4. admit, refuse, defer, branch, or escalate;
  5. lower through an admitted compiler or transformation regime;
  6. mediate each external effect at its grant and invocation boundary;
  7. record result, partial failure, rollback, and residual state;
  8. verify claimed properties under a declared observation regime; and
  9. revise without erasing predecessor custody.

The path does not make a proposal correct. It makes the conditions under which it can bind inspectable.

5. Negative Power and Refusal

Open-source sovereignty includes the ability to inspect, modify, fork, and exit. Jnana adds negative power: the ability of a user, maintainer, policy, or constitutional layer to prevent a proposed transformation from binding.

Refusal must be more than an error code. It should bind:

  • the exact proposal and requested capability;
  • the law and authority under which it was evaluated;
  • the reason and evidence class;
  • whether revision, appeal, alternate realization, or branch is available; and
  • whether any effect occurred before refusal.

Negative power becomes hollow if maintainers cannot inspect the law, providers can change hidden dependencies, emergency paths bypass receipts, or generated artifacts cannot be reconstructed.

6. Operating-System Integration Is a Profile, Not the Kernel

Jnana need not replace Linux or become a monolithic kernel. It can be realized as profiles around selected transformation and effect boundaries:

  • source and dependency intake;
  • build and package execution;
  • module or extension admission;
  • service installation and activation;
  • agent tool grants;
  • filesystem, process, network, and device effects;
  • configuration-to-execution transitions; and
  • update, rollback, repair, and recovery.

Kernel configuration in MCIR, gated module insertion, declared syscall surfaces, and AI-proposed patches are candidate configurations. They remain hypothetical until a concrete boundary, threat model, integration design, and court of tests exist.

No constitutional layer can govern effects it cannot observe or mediate. Lower firmware, management engines, peripheral controllers, cloud services, and physical access remain outside the claim unless incorporated into the verified boundary.

7. Hardware and Provider Sovereignty

Hardware sovereignty is not achieved by hashing firmware alone. A trustworthy profile may require:

  • reproducible or independently reconstructable artifacts;
  • measured and verified boot where supported;
  • explicit backend, ABI, instruction, device, and firmware assumptions;
  • signed provenance whose key authority is governed;
  • update and rollback policy;
  • anti-rollback or explicit downgrade semantics;
  • physical and recovery threat boundaries; and
  • evidence that the realized hardware behavior corresponds to the declared artifact.

Provider sovereignty likewise requires exportable source, law, schemas, identity data, receipts, dependencies, and reconstruction instructions. A portable receipt that still depends on a proprietary unavailable model or service does not establish provider exit.

8. What Jnana Adds to Open Source

Jnana’s strongest proposed contribution is not simply machine-checkable governance. It is a collaboration surface in which contributors can inspect and contest:

  • transformation regimes and compiler passes;
  • capability and effect classes;
  • authority and delegation;
  • identity and equivalence law;
  • constitutional invariants and amendment rules;
  • evidence and replay classifications;
  • exceptions, break-glass events, and recovery; and
  • the correspondence between source, artifact, and realized effect.

This can make authority more visible. It can also increase complexity, centralize schema control, exclude contributors, or encode a flawed constitution more durably. The architecture must preserve human contestability and forkability rather than replacing social governance with an opaque policy engine.

9. Incremental Adoption Ladder

The source presents an integrated vision. Adoption need not begin with a complete operating system.

  • Level: 0
    Bounded adoption target: exact source/result custody and receipts
    Claim ceiling: traceability only

  • Level: 1
    Bounded adoption target: capability/effect inventory and visible grants
    Claim ceiling: observed inventory, not complete mediation

  • Level: 2
    Bounded adoption target: proposal/approval separation and refusal
    Claim ceiling: bounded authority separation

  • Level: 3
    Bounded adoption target: admitted transformation regime with MCIR artifacts
    Claim ceiling: conformance only for tested transformations

  • Level: 4
    Bounded adoption target: mediated build, package, service, and agent effects
    Claim ceiling: coverage limited to demonstrated paths

  • Level: 5
    Bounded adoption target: OS and hardware integration profile
    Claim ceiling: assurance limited to verified platform boundary

Each level needs its own evidence. A project should not claim Level 5 because it has Level 0 receipts.

$$ \begin{aligned} &P \\ &\rightarrow S \\ &\rightarrow CM_1 \\ &\rightarrow CM_2 \\ &\rightarrow IC \\ &\rightarrow IR \\ &\rightarrow E \\ &\rightarrow R \end{aligned}. $$

12. Evaluation Program

The proposal should be evaluated through: (1) untrusted AI proposals; (2) dependency insertion; (3) file, process, network, credential, and device effects; (4) build and installation hooks; (5) denied and deferred capability; (6) direct-path and emergency bypass attempts; (7) partial failure and rollback; (8) deterministic and equivalence-class replay; (9) compiler and policy upgrades; (10) provider removal and reconstruction; (11) firmware and boot boundaries; (12) contributor comprehension; (13) maintainership and fork governance; (14) accessibility and latency; and (15) adversarial law capture.

Open-Source Sovereignty Requires Mediation of Every Relevant Effect Path

Public orientation

Jnana proposes a constitutional transformation substrate for AI-assisted software: AI proposes; governed representations carry commitments; versioned law evaluates them; admitted machinery lowers them; effect boundaries mediate them; and receipts preserve lineage. The proposal extends open-source sovereignty only where its controls actually cover the relevant paths. Source visibility, a policy file, or a receipt does not by itself contain an agent.

Adversarial tests

  1. Ambient-capability attack. Shells, package managers, networks, credentials, editors, caches, agents, installers, firmware, or humans bypass the declared transformation path.
  2. Representation-bypass attack. A proposal reaches code or effect without passing through the governed intermediate form and admission gates.
  3. Policy-self-amendment attack. The same actor or compromised path can alter the law, evaluator, adapter, tests, exceptions, and evidence that constrain it.
  4. Receipt-containment attack. A complete trace is mistaken for prevention, safe execution, correct effect, or successful rollback.
  5. Replay-closure attack. Deterministic replay is claimed while tools, dependencies, services, clocks, randomness, hardware, firmware, or side effects remain open.
  6. Provider-exit attack. Exported artifacts preserve code but lose model behavior, policy semantics, histories, secrets, build state, or practical reconstructability.
  7. Open-source-displacement attack. Constitutional machinery centralizes contribution, raises participation cost, or weakens existing maintainer and community governance.
  8. Hardware-sovereignty attack. Inspectable software is described as sovereign beyond unverified chips, firmware, boot chains, peripherals, and manufacturing dependencies.

Claim and evidence ceiling

The paper specifies a constitutional transformation architecture and incremental adoption program. It does not establish complete AI containment, universal mediation, a working operating-system layer, reproducible execution, hardware sovereignty, provider independence, security, usability, lower cost, or a new open-source revolution. Jnana does not replace Git, Linux, maintainers, licensing, testing, review, or social governance.

Every containment claim requires enumerated effect paths, enforced isolation or mediation, bypass testing, exact environment closure, recovery and provider-exit exercises, community and usability evidence, threat modeling, and current systems and open-source research. No implementation, deployment, or external research was performed in this pass.

Reader-facing consequence

The credible promise is that AI-generated transformation can become inspectable, negotiable, refuseable, reconstructable, and portable - but only to the exact boundary where every consequential path has been shown to obey the constitution.

15. Conclusion

The first open-source revolution opened code. An AI-accelerated software culture also needs to open transformation authority: who proposed a change, which law applied, which capability was granted, which effects occurred, what evidence supports the result, and whether users can refuse, reconstruct, fork, and exit.

Jnana offers a coherent proposal for that extension. Its constitutional value does not arise from attaching AI to an operating system. It arises from keeping AI output subordinate to inspectable authority, mediated capability, bounded effect, receipt, and revision.

That promise remains conditional. Jnana becomes a sovereignty mechanism only where its boundaries are actually enforced and its costs, exclusions, bypasses, and dependencies remain visible. The next open-source revolution would not merely make power inspectable. It would make power contestable, reconstructable, and refusable.
