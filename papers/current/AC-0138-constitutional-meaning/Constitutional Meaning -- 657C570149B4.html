Constitutional Meaning

Meaning-Binding, Participation, and the Structure of Admissible Futures

A distinction means something when it changes what may continue.

Author
Adam Ableman
Constitutional Computing
Contact: ableman.research@gmail.com

Abstract

This paper presents a constitutional theory of meaning.

Most theories treat meaning as representation, interpretation, reference, symbol grounding, or semantic correspondence. Meaning is generally understood as a relationship between a sign and what the sign denotes.

The constitutional perspective begins elsewhere.

The framework proposes that meaning arises whenever a distinction changes admissible continuation. Meaning is not fundamentally representational. Meaning is structural.

Under this view, meaning exists wherever distinctions alter the space of possible futures. The same principle applies across biological, computational, social, and cognitive systems. A signal, symbol, event, memory, observation, or concept becomes meaningful when it changes what may happen next.

The paper introduces:

  • constitutional meaning,
  • meaning-binding,
  • continuation sensitivity,
  • semantic participation,
  • constitutional semantics.

The framework provides a substrate-neutral account of meaning capable of spanning organisms, institutions, machines, and collectives without requiring commitment to a specific representational theory.

The central claim:

Meaning is the capacity of a distinction to alter admissible continuation.

1. Introduction

The dominant question of semantics has historically been:

What does a symbol refer to?

This question appears in many forms:

  • What does a word mean?
  • What does a signal represent?
  • How do symbols acquire content?
  • How is reference grounded?
  • How does information become meaningful?

Most theories begin with relationships between signs and objects.

The constitutional perspective begins elsewhere.

It asks:

What changes because this distinction exists?

The focus shifts from reference to consequence.

Meaning becomes less about what a distinction describes and more about what a distinction does.

Across nature and engineering, distinctions routinely influence behavior:

  • immune recognition,
  • predator detection,
  • routing signals,
  • market prices,
  • linguistic symbols,
  • institutional rules.

The common structure may not be representation.

The common structure may be altered continuation.

Semantic participation therefore precedes interpretation.

Constitutional semantics studies relationships between:

  • distinctions,

8. Meaning Without Interpretation

Interpretation may occur.

Interpretation is not required.

  • immune recognition,

  • collision avoidance.

These systems exhibit meaningful distinctions.

Yet no explicit interpretation may be present.

The distinction alters continuation.

Meaning exists regardless.

9. Meaning and Information

Information and meaning are distinct.

A distinction may carry information without changing continuation.

In such cases:

M(Δ) = 0.

The distinction is informational but constitutionally meaningless.

Conversely, a small informational signal may dramatically alter admissibility.

Meaning therefore cannot be reduced to information volume.

10. Meaning and Truth

The framework separates meaning from truth.

A distinction may be:

  • true and meaningful,
  • false and meaningful,
  • true and meaningless,
  • false and meaningless.

Meaning concerns continuation.

Truth concerns correspondence.

The two relations are independent.

A false alarm may alter behavior substantially.

Its meaning may be large despite its falsity.

11. Meaning and Value

Meaning is not equivalent to value.

A distinction may alter continuation without improving outcomes.

Similarly:

A valuable distinction may possess little semantic influence.

The constitutional framework therefore separates:

  • meaning,
  • value,
  • truth.

These quantities may interact.

They are not identical.

18. Propositions

Proposition 1 (Meaning-Binding Criterion)

A distinction possesses constitutional meaning if and only if:

AΔ, t△At ≠ ∅.

Proof Sketch

If admissibility changes, continuation changes.

If continuation changes, future structure changes.

The distinction therefore influences the future and is meaningful.

Conversely, if admissibility remains unchanged, continuation remains unchanged.

The distinction has no constitutional effect.

Proposition 2 (Semantic Composition)

Assume:

Δ1

and

Δ2

possess constitutional meaning.

Then joint meaning need not equal additive meaning.

Proof Sketch

The continuation effects of distinctions may overlap, reinforce, or cancel.

Meaning composition is therefore generally non-linear.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a constitutional theory of meaning.

Meaning is not treated as representation, interpretation, or reference alone.

Instead, meaning is treated as the capacity of a distinction to alter admissible continuation.

The framework introduces constitutional meaning, meaning-binding, continuation sensitivity, semantic participation, and constitutional semantics as tools for describing this phenomenon.

Meaning becomes measurable through its influence on future structure.

Representation may exist.

Interpretation may exist.

Neither is fundamental.

The central hypothesis:

Meaning corresponds to the alteration of admissible continuation rather than the existence of representation alone.

If correct, meaning appears wherever distinctions shape futures.

The symbol is only one participant in a much larger semantic universe.
