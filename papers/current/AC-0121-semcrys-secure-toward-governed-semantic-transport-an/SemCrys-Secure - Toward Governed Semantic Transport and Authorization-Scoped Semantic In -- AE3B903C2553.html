SemCrys-Secure: Toward Governed Semantic Transport and Authorization-Scoped Semantic Interpretability for Machine Cognition

Rotating Semantic Encoding, Semantic Access Control, and Cryptographically Governed Meaning Pathways for Large Language Model Communication

Reconstructing the Relationship Between Meaning, Authorization, and Semantic Transport Through Keyed Semantic Symbol Systems

Author
Adam Ableman
Constitutional Computing
Contact: ableman.research@gmail.com

Abstract

Large language model communication currently assumes that semantic interpretation is universally accessible to any observer capable of reading the representational surface. Existing security systems primarily protect byte-level transport through encryption while leaving semantic structure itself largely ungoverned once decrypted. Simultaneously, recent work in LLM steganography and covert communication demonstrates that language models can participate in hidden communication channels embedded within natural language generation.

This paper introduces SemCrys-Secure, a governed semantic transport framework in which semantic interpretation pathways become cryptographically mediated, rotation-aware, and authorization-scoped. Emerging from the semantic density amplification mechanisms demonstrated by SemCrys0 and the semantic-native representation framework proposed by SemCrys, SemCrys-Secure extends symbolic semantic systems into keyed semantic encoding and governed semantic access control.

Unlike conventional encryption systems, SemCrys-Secure does not attempt to replace cryptographic confidentiality primitives. Instead, it introduces a semantic-layer access system in which semantic interpretation itself depends upon possession of authorized semantic interfaces, codebooks, key schedules, and admissible semantic pathways. Semantic bindings become rotational, contextual, and authorization-dependent rather than globally fixed.

The paper formalizes keyed semantic transport, semantic codebook rotation, semantic admissibility pathways, semantic access control, rotating semantic topology, semantic residue minimization, and constitutional semantic governance. It further distinguishes semantic transport security from traditional cryptographic confidentiality while arguing that meaning-access itself constitutes a governable computational surface.

SemCrys-Secure does not claim semantic indistinguishability under conventional cryptographic threat models. Given sufficient access to semantic interfaces, model outputs, training distributions, or interaction pathways, adversaries may eventually infer semantic structure statistically. The framework instead seeks to increase inference cost, reduce passive accessibility, constrain reliable interpretation pathways, and introduce authorization-scoped semantic mediation into machine communication systems.

The central claim of SemCrys-Secure is that semantic interpretation itself may become an authorization-controlled operation, enabling new classes of machine-native semantic access systems without reducing cryptographic security to mere obscurity.

1. Introduction

Modern secure communication systems primarily protect:

  • byte transport,
  • message confidentiality,
  • authentication,
  • and integrity.

Once decrypted, however, semantic interpretation becomes universally accessible to any observer capable of reading the representational surface.

Simultaneously, large language models increasingly participate in:

  • autonomous communication,
  • agent orchestration,
  • machine-to-machine interaction,
  • and semantic coordination systems.

This creates a new problem domain:

semantic access control.

SemCrys-Secure explores whether semantic interpretation itself may become:

  • authorization-scoped,
  • pathway-governed,
  • and cryptographically mediated.

Rather than securing only byte transport, SemCrys-Secure investigates:

  • governed semantic transport.

The framework emerges from two prior observations:

  1. SemCrys0 demonstrated that semantic density amplification can emerge through symbolic protocol engineering alone.
  2. SemCrys proposed that stabilized semantic structures may become tokenizer-native representational objects.

SemCrys-Secure extends these ideas into:

  • rotating semantic encoding,
  • keyed semantic addressability,
  • and authorization-dependent semantic interpretation pathways.

The framework may be understood as:

  • a semantic-layer analogue of capability-based access control, where:
  • possession of authorized semantic interfaces determines the ability to reliably resolve meaning.

In this view:

  • semantic interfaces function as capabilities granting access to specific semantic resolution pathways.

We define authorization-scoped semantic interpretability as:

the property that reliable semantic resolution depends upon possession of valid semantic interfaces and admissible decoding pathways.

Operationally, this means that unauthorized observers may still perceive representational surfaces while lacking access to:

  • stable,
  • low-ambiguity semantic reconstruction.

2. Historical Lineage

2.1 Classical Cryptography

Classical cryptographic systems evolved from:

  • monoalphabetic substitution,
  • polyalphabetic ciphers,
  • rotating alphabets,
  • and keyed transformational mappings.

These systems sought to prevent unauthorized semantic recovery through controlled representational transformation.

SemCrys-Secure shares structural similarities with polyalphabetic systems:

  • semantic mappings rotate,
  • symbol assignments vary,
  • and repeated semantic structures need not preserve stable surface representations.

However, SemCrys-Secure differs fundamentally in domain.

Its target is not merely:

  • encrypted text, but:
  • semantic interpretation pathways themselves.

2.2 Steganography and Hidden Communication

Steganographic systems attempt to conceal the existence of communication itself.

Recent LLM steganography research demonstrates that language models may embed covert signals inside seemingly ordinary language generation.

SemCrys-Secure intersects with this domain but differs in purpose.

Its primary goal is not concealment alone, but:

  • governed semantic accessibility,
  • semantic pathway authorization,
  • and semantic interpretation control.

The system therefore functions less as pure steganography and more as:

  • semantic access architecture.

2.3 Pasigraphy and Symbolic Language Systems

SemCrys-Secure also inherits from symbolic language systems and pasigraphy.

However, traditional pasigraphic systems assumed:

  • universal readability.

SemCrys-Secure instead introduces:

  • scoped readability,
  • rotating semantic bindings,
  • and pathway-dependent interpretation.

Meaning therefore becomes:

  • interface-dependent, rather than:
  • globally recoverable.

SemCrys0 provides:

  • session-local symbolic substitution,

Its mappings are generally:

  • static within the session,

  • and surface-oriented.

SemCrys extends symbolic compression into:

  • tokenizer-native semantic representation,
  • semantic cooling,
  • and stabilized semantic atoms.

Semantic structures become:

  • representational primitives, rather than:
  • temporary substitutions.

3.3 SemCrys-Secure

SemCrys-Secure extends symbolic semantic systems into:

  • keyed semantic transport,
  • rotating semantic pathways,
  • authorization-dependent interpretation,
  • and governed semantic access.

Semantic interpretation itself becomes:

  • pathway-scoped,
  • rotation-aware,
  • and authorization-mediated.

The three systems form a layered progression:

  • SemCrys0 — symbolic compression
  • SemCrys — semantic-native representation
  • SemCrys-Secure — governed semantic transport

4. Governed Semantic Transport

4.1 Definition

Governed semantic transport is a communication framework in which:

  • semantic interpretation depends upon authorized access to semantic pathways, rotational mappings, and admissible decoding structures.

Meaning-access therefore becomes:

  • conditional, rather than:
  • universally recoverable.

The system does not attempt to make semantic leakage impossible. Instead, it attempts to restrict:

  • stable,
  • reliable,
  • and operationally useful interpretation pathways.

More precisely, the goal is to restrict:

  • stable,
  • low-ambiguity semantic reconstruction

for unauthorized observers.

4.2 Semantic Interfaces

SemCrys-Secure introduces semantic interfaces:

  • governed semantic interpretation layers associated with particular codebooks, pathways, or semantic projections.

Possession of the correct semantic interface determines whether:

  • semantic structures resolve coherently, or:
  • collapse into semantically unstable residue.

A semantic interface may be implemented as:

  • a versioned, authenticated codebook bundle containing:

    • mapping functions,
    • rotational schedules,
    • admissibility constraints,
    • and semantic pathway metadata.

Semantic interfaces may therefore include:

  • symbol mappings,
  • rotational schedules,
  • semantic admissibility rules,
  • pathway identifiers,
  • and contextual decoding constraints.

4.3 Keyed Semantic Addressability

SemCrys-Secure introduces keyed semantic addressability:

  • semantic resolution pathways derived from cryptographic key material.

A semantic mapping may therefore depend upon:

session_key + nonce + message_counter + recipient_scope + domain_id

The same concept need not preserve the same symbolic surface across sessions, turns, or recipients.

For example:

Session “receipt”
A Æ
B Δ7
C κ4

derived from distinct semantic rotation schedules via a deterministic, key-seeded mapping function.

This prevents stable semantic frequency accumulation across large traffic volumes.

5. Rotating Semantic Encoding

5.1 Static vs Rotating Mappings

Static semantic substitution systems eventually leak statistical structure.

Repeated semantic recurrence creates:

  • frequency signatures,
  • semantic recovery opportunities,
  • and codebook inference pathways.

SemCrys-Secure therefore introduces:

  • rotational semantic encoding.

Mappings may vary:

  • per message,
  • per session,
  • per recipient,
  • or per semantic domain.

5.2 Rotational Semantic Topology

Semantic rotation may operate across multiple layers:

  • Symbol rotation — semantic atom → different symbols
  • Phrase rotation — alternate semantic structures
  • Structural rotation — syntactic reorganization
  • Pathway rotation — alternate semantic interfaces
  • Domain rotation — context-scoped semantic projections

In practice, implementations may select only a subset of these layers depending upon operational constraints. Symbol-level and phrase-level rotation constitute the minimal viable system.

This reduces persistent semantic surface stability available to adversarial observers.

5.3 Semantic Residue Minimization

SemCrys-Secure attempts to minimize:

  • stable semantic residue.

Semantic residue refers to:

  • persistent statistical patterns,
  • recurring semantic surfaces,
  • or recoverable semantic regularities

remaining visible across traffic.

Semantic residue may be approximated through observable stability metrics such as:

  • cross-message symbol recurrence,
  • n-gram persistence,
  • or mutual information between surface representations and inferred semantic clusters.

Rotation systems therefore seek:

  • pattern destabilization,
  • semantic frequency flattening,
  • and semantic ambiguity amplification for unauthorized observers.

6. Cryptography vs Semantic Security

6.1 SemCrys-Secure is Not Encryption

SemCrys-Secure does not replace conventional cryptographic systems.

Cryptographic confidentiality remains necessary for:

  • transport security,
  • authentication,
  • integrity,
  • and provable confidentiality guarantees.

SemCrys-Secure instead operates as:

  • a semantic-layer authorization system.

SemCrys-Secure composes with conventional encryption by operating strictly after decryption, introducing a second-stage control over semantic accessibility rather than transport confidentiality.

6.2 Semantic Security Layer

SemCrys-Secure introduces a second layer:

  • Cryptographic layer — protects bytes
  • Semantic layer — governs meaning access

The semantic layer determines:

  • whether semantic interpretation becomes operationally accessible.

While superficially similar to obfuscation, SemCrys-Secure differs in that semantic transformation is systematically governed, key-derived, and pathway-constrained rather than ad hoc or purely concealment-oriented.

6.3 Security Boundaries

A fixed semantic codebook alone is not cryptographically secure.

Attackers possessing:

  • plaintext/ciphertext pairs,
  • sufficient traffic,
  • model access,
  • semantic interfaces,
  • or semantic frequency observations

may eventually infer mappings statistically.

SemCrys-Secure therefore does not claim semantic indistinguishability under conventional cryptographic threat models.

Instead, the framework seeks to:

  • increase semantic inference cost,
  • reduce passive interpretability,
  • destabilize statistical semantic recovery,
  • and constrain reliable interpretation pathways through governed semantic mediation.

In this sense, SemCrys-Secure operates not by eliminating information leakage entirely, but by reducing exploitable mutual information between observable representations and recoverable semantic structure for unauthorized observers.

Security therefore depends upon:

  • cryptographic key schedules,
  • rotational mapping dynamics,
  • semantic pathway governance,
  • authenticated semantic interfaces,
  • and admissibility-scoped interpretation systems.

7. Semantic Governance and Admissibility

7.1 Meaning as a Governed Surface

SemCrys-Secure treats meaning-access itself as a governed operation.

Semantic resolution becomes:

  • receiptable,
  • pathway-scoped,
  • and admissibility-gated.

7.2 Semantic Admissibility

Semantic interpretation pathways may depend upon:

  • authorization scope,
  • recipient identity,
  • semantic domain,
  • pathway legitimacy,
  • or governance rules.

Operationally, inadmissible semantic requests may result in:

  • degraded interpretation,
  • ambiguous semantic residue,
  • refusal behavior,
  • or intentionally incomplete semantic recovery.

This creates:

  • semantic constitutionalization.

Meaning-access itself becomes subject to governance constraints.

7.3 Semantic Poisoning and Manipulation

SemCrys-Secure must resist:

  • semantic codebook poisoning,
  • adversarial remapping,
  • semantic replay attacks,
  • unauthorized stabilization,
  • and semantic pathway hijacking.

Governed semantic transport therefore requires:

  • authenticated semantic state,
  • pathway verification,
  • and admissible semantic mutation rules.

9. Threat Model

9.1 Threat Classes

SemCrys-Secure considers:

  • statistical codebook inference,
  • known-plaintext attacks,
  • semantic traffic analysis,
  • semantic pathway discovery,
  • semantic replay attacks,
  • model compromise,
  • semantic residue accumulation,
  • adaptive query attacks,
  • prompt probing,
  • and interaction-based semantic inference.

An attacker is considered successful if they can:

  • reconstruct stable semantic mappings,
  • or reliably infer semantic intent across messages

without authorized semantic interfaces.

9.2 Traffic Analysis

Even when semantic mappings rotate, adversaries may still infer information through:

  • timing,
  • traffic volume,
  • semantic recurrence,
  • message length,
  • and interaction structure.

SemCrys-Secure therefore cannot eliminate all side-channel leakage.

9.3 Model-in-the-Loop Adversaries

Large language model systems introduce a distinct threat class:

  • adversaries capable of repeatedly querying models to infer semantic mappings interactively.

Attackers may attempt:

  • prompt probing,
  • semantic elicitation,
  • statistical semantic reconstruction,
  • or iterative mapping discovery.

This creates a new category of semantic attack surface absent from classical static code systems.

9.4 Model Compromise

If an adversary gains access to:

  • the authorized model,
  • semantic interfaces,
  • rotational key schedules,
  • or pathway-governance state,

security may collapse.

SemCrys-Secure therefore assumes:

  • secure key management,
  • governance enforcement,
  • and pathway isolation remain necessary.

10. Prototype Implementation Pathway

A minimal SemCrys-Secure prototype pathway may proceed incrementally:

  1. Begin with SemCrys0 symbolic substitution.
  2. Introduce cryptographically seeded rotational mappings.
  3. Derive symbol schedules from shared key material and nonce state.
  4. Implement semantic interface identifiers.
  5. Introduce pathway-scoped semantic admissibility rules.
  6. Add authenticated semantic state receipts.
  7. Layer semantic-native tokenization through SemCrys semantic atoms.
  8. Introduce governed semantic pathway rotation and interface negotiation.

Correct operation requires synchronized semantic interfaces and rotation schedules between communicating parties, introducing coordination and state-management overhead analogous to key synchronization in cryptographic systems.

SemCrys-Secure therefore evolves incrementally from symbolic semantic compression toward governed semantic transport.

11. Limitations

SemCrys-Secure introduces substantial unresolved challenges:

  • semantic residue leakage,
  • traffic analysis exposure,
  • model compromise risk,
  • semantic governance complexity,
  • pathway synchronization overhead,
  • adversarial semantic inference,
  • semantic replay vulnerabilities,
  • and semantic-interface management difficulty.

The framework also risks:

  • semantic fragmentation,
  • interoperability collapse,
  • governance centralization,
  • semantic overfitting,
  • and over-complex semantic ecosystems.

SemCrys-Secure therefore should not be treated as a replacement for conventional cryptography.

12. Discussion

SemCrys-Secure suggests that future communication systems may increasingly distinguish between:

  • byte accessibility, and:
  • semantic accessibility.

This reframes part of the security problem away from:

  • transport secrecy alone,

toward:

  • governed meaning access.

The framework therefore belongs simultaneously to:

  • semantic systems,
  • cryptographic systems,
  • machine communication,
  • cognitive architecture,
  • symbolic representation,
  • semantic governance theory,
  • and authorization systems.

SemCrys-Secure does not propose that meaning can become perfectly hidden through semantic rotation alone.

Instead, it proposes that semantic interpretation itself may become:

  • authorization-scoped,
  • pathway-governed,
  • and computationally mediated.

13. Conclusion

SemCrys-Secure proposes that semantic interpretation itself may become a governed computational surface through keyed semantic transport, rotating semantic encoding, and authorization-dependent semantic interfaces.

Emerging from the symbolic compression mechanisms of SemCrys0 and the semantic-native representation framework of SemCrys, SemCrys-Secure extends semantic systems into semantic access architecture.

The framework distinguishes:

  • cryptographic confidentiality, from:
  • semantic accessibility.

Its central claim is simple:

access to meaning may itself become governable.
