## Abstract

Institutions depend on revisable semantic anchors: definitions, classifications, measures, precedents, evidentiary rules, and authority relations that connect claims to observation and consequence. Those anchors must change when evidence, conditions, or legitimate purposes change. They can also be changed in ways that preserve procedural legitimacy while concealing weakened correspondence, displaced accountability, or asymmetric advantage.

This paper develops **ontology laundering** as a candidate diagnostic for the latter pattern. Ontology laundering is not every semantic change, mistaken definition, political disagreement, institutional failure, or disputed measurement. It is a materially consequential alteration of an interpretive system whose provenance, authority, evidentiary basis, consequences, or contestability are obscured in a way that makes a previously visible conflict appear resolved, admissible, or authoritative.

The framework separates deliberate laundering from emergent drift, negligence, adaptive revision, and good-faith disagreement. It models an ontology version, a change event, and a multidimensional risk profile; provides tests for vocabulary drift, metric substitution, procedural displacement, model and statistical laundering, synthetic evidence, consensus closure, and legitimacy recursion; and identifies false-positive controls. The paper integrates Meaning as Binding, Semantic Debt, Governed Semantic Cartography, Authority Laundering Theory, Reasoning by Way of Receipts, Governance Coverage Gaps, the Governed Meaning Base, and the universal GSM.

The result is a research and governance specification, not a finding that any named institution has engaged in laundering. No external case research was conducted. The framework requires exact change custody, competing explanations, asymmetric-effect analysis, contradiction channels, outcome evidence, and explicit limits before it can support a bounded diagnosis.

## 1. The Problem

Most accounts of institutional corruption assume that a stable rule or meaning has been violated. A record is falsified, a procedure bypassed, an authority forged, or evidence concealed. These cases remain legible because the semantic anchors by which the act is judged still have force.

Ontology laundering concerns a different failure surface: the anchors themselves change. A word is broadened, an outcome is replaced by a proxy, a verification practice becomes a credential, an authority validates its own standards, or an exclusion rule removes the observations by which the arrangement could fail. The change may be formally approved and internally coherent. The question is whether the transition remains visibly and corrigibly coupled to its declared purpose, evidence, and affected parties.

The theory is useful only if it can distinguish legitimate revision from semantic change that hides its own burden.

## 2. Definitions

An **ontology** here is not a complete metaphysics. It is a bounded institutional interpretation system:

$$
O_t=(V_t,R_t,A_t,P_t,M_t),
$$

where $V_t$ is vocabulary, $R_t$ typed relations and classifications, $A_t$ semantic anchors, $P_t$ interpretive and admissibility procedures, and $M_t$ measures or proxies at version $t$.

A **semantic anchor** is a governed relation connecting a symbol or classification to some combination of observation, authority, prior usage, procedure, consequence, and revision condition. An anchor is never perfect access to reality. It is an inspectable correspondence claim with a bounded observation regime.

An **ontology change** is a transition $O_t\rightarrow O_{t+1}$. It may be corrective, adaptive, contested, negligent, captured, or deliberately deceptive.

**Ontology laundering** is a candidate classification for a materially consequential ontology change that:

1. changes what can be asserted, measured, admitted, refused, or authorized;
2. obscures or misrepresents the change, its authority, evidentiary burden, beneficiaries, losses, or alternatives; and
3. converts that opacity into apparent legitimacy, closure, or exemption from contradiction.

The definition does not require an advance finding of malicious intent. Intent is a separate evidentiary question.

## 3. Legitimate Revision and Laundering Risk

Semantic stability is not semantic immobility. A healthy institution must correct obsolete classifications, harmful language, inaccurate measures, new evidence, and changed operating conditions. Legitimate revision can be controversial and still be legitimate.

A revision has stronger standing when it preserves:

- exact predecessor and successor definitions;
- declared change authority and scope;
- stated reasons and supporting evidence;
- mappings between old and new terms;
- known incompatibilities and migration effects;
- affected-party and conflict disclosures;
- admissible contradiction and appeal;
- external outcome checks;
- reversible or compensating paths where feasible; and
- revision when the new anchor fails.

Ontology laundering risk rises when these are absent, especially when absence benefits the authority responsible for the change. No single missing field proves laundering.

## 4. The Ontology Change Record

Represent a proposed change as:

$$
\Delta_t=(O_t,O_{t+1},p,u,a,q,e,b,x,k),
$$

where $p$ is the proposer, $u$ the authorizing authority, $a$ the affected anchors, $q$ the declared rationale, $e$ supporting and counterevidence, $b$ beneficiaries and burdened parties, $x$ expected and observed effects, and $k$ contest, compatibility, and rollback provisions.

The record should bind both semantic and operational effects. A definition change can alter eligibility, safety classifications, funding, moderation, legal consequence, scientific inclusion, or model behavior. A formally complete receipt proves only that the recorded transition occurred under the recorded process. It does not prove that the new ontology is true, fair, complete, or legitimate.

## 5. A Risk Vector, Not an Oracle

Ontology laundering should not be reduced to one accusation score. Use a profile:

$$
L(\Delta_t)=(o,c,d,r,s,g,v),
$$

where:

- $o$ is change opacity;
- $c$ is authority concentration or conflict;
- $d$ is decoupling from declared evidence or outcomes;
- $r$ is loss of reversibility or compatibility;
- $s$ is suppression of contradiction or appeal;
- $g$ is proxy gaming or metric displacement; and
- $v$ is self-validation or legitimacy recursion.

Each dimension requires a declared scale, evidence source, uncertainty, and reviewer. The vector supports comparison and inquiry; it does not create an automatic verdict. Different domains may weight dimensions differently, and some necessary changes are irreversible without being laundered.

## 6. Classification Without Premature Intent Attribution

The same observed pattern can have different causes. A review should distinguish at least:

- **documented legitimate revision:** material change with visible authority, evidence, consequences, and contest;
- **contested revision:** visible change with unresolved value or evidence disputes;
- **under-governed drift:** change accumulated without adequate custody or review;
- **negligent distortion:** foreseeable semantic or outcome damage ignored or inadequately tested;
- **strategic laundering candidate:** opacity or closure plausibly used to secure legitimacy or advantage; and
- **deliberate laundering finding:** intent supported by evidence independent of the resulting pattern.

Only the last category makes an intent finding. Outcome, incentive, and asymmetry may justify investigation but do not substitute for evidence of intent.

## 7. Mechanism Families

The source identifies several mechanism families. They are best treated as testable patterns.

**Vocabulary drift** occurs when a term's extension, threshold, or normative force changes without adequate versioning or migration. Drift may be adaptive; risk appears when material consequences are hidden behind continuity of wording.

**Metric substitution** occurs when a proxy becomes the operational objective and failure against the original purpose is no longer visible. Proxy use is unavoidable in many domains; the test is whether the proxy's limits, gaming surface, and outcome relation remain inspectable.

**Procedural displacement** occurs when completing an admissibility process is treated as sufficient evidence for the underlying claim. Procedure can warrant a decision without establishing truth.

**Model laundering** occurs when a model's assumptions, data, uncertainty, fitness domain, or failure record are obscured while the output gains authority beyond demonstrated support. Model transparency alone is insufficient: privacy, security, proprietary restrictions, and comprehension costs may require controlled access and independent evaluation.

**Statistical laundering** concerns analytic choices that produce a materially misleading but procedurally defensible result. Endpoint, denominator, exclusion, aggregation, uncertainty, and multiplicity choices require domain-specific evidence; their mere presence is not laundering.

**Synthetic evidence laundering** occurs when generated, transformed, relabeled, or recursively cited material is presented with stronger independence or empirical standing than it possesses.

## 8. Consensus and Legitimacy Recursion

Consensus can summarize convergent evidence, coordinate action under uncertainty, or record professional judgment. It is not inherently a laundering mechanism. Risk appears when the existence of consensus replaces the evidence for the claim, dissent is excluded from the evidence process by definition, or the same authority controls membership, standards, validation, and appeal.

Legitimacy recursion occurs when an institution's authority is justified primarily by outputs, credentials, models, or procedures whose validity depends on that same authority. Some recursion is unavoidable in constitutional and professional systems. The diagnostic question is whether independent constraint, plural review, contest, and revision can enter the loop.

## 9. Civilization-Scale Ontology Capture

The source proposes that mutually reinforcing institutions can converge on a self-protective semantic structure. This is a large hypothesis, not an established result. Apparent cross-domain convergence may instead reflect shared evidence, law, infrastructure, culture, coordination needs, market concentration, or observer selection.

The hypothesis becomes researchable only with explicit domains, time windows, anchor changes, dependency relations, counterexamples, external outcome measures, and alternate causal models. "Civilization-scale capture" should not function as an explanation that absorbs all disagreement.

## 10. Diagnostic Protocol

For a bounded suspected change:

1. identify the exact predecessor and successor ontology versions;
2. enumerate changed terms, relations, measures, procedures, and consequences;
3. identify proposer, authority, affected parties, and declared purpose;
4. reconstruct evidence, counterevidence, uncertainty, and excluded material;
5. test compatibility, migration, appeal, and revision paths;
6. compare observed outcomes against the old and new declared purposes;
7. identify benefits, burdens, conflicts, and asymmetric enforcement;
8. score the risk vector with uncertainty and reviewer disagreement;
9. test benign and legitimate alternate explanations;
10. classify the case without inferring intent beyond the evidence; and
11. issue a revisable finding bound to exact sources and observation limits.

The protocol can return   
`INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE`, `LEGITIMATE_REVISION`,  
`CONTESTED_REVISION`, `UNDER_GOVERNED_DRIFT`, `LAUNDERING_RISK`,  
or a more specific finding authorized by the domain. Refusal to classify is a valid result.

## 11. GMB and GSM Integration

Inclusion in GMB is custody and navigability, not truth or standing.

The universal **GSM** can type relations such as `REVISES`, `REPLACES_ANCHOR`, `USES_PROXY`,

## Diagnose Strategic Anchor Change Without Criminalizing Disagreement

### Public orientation

Ontology laundering names a specific adversarial pattern: the semantic substrate used to judge conduct changes so strategic behavior becomes legitimate in appearance while reality coupling and contradiction permeability deteriorate. The framework does not classify ordinary disagreement, conceptual innovation, democratic revision, or institutional learning as corruption.

### Adversarial tests

1. **Legitimate-evolution attack.** A necessary conceptual correction is mislabeled laundering because it challenges incumbents. The diagnostic must test authority, evidence, effects, and openness to contradiction.
2. **Partisan-accusation attack.** Opponents use the label to delegitimize each other without a bounded docket. Claims require exact before/after anchors and behavioral consequences.
3. **Consensus-as-proof attack.** Broad adoption is treated as legitimacy, or minority status as evidence of laundering. Consensus and warrant remain separate.
4. **Anchor-selection attack.** The analyst chooses a favorable historical baseline. Baseline authority, date, alternatives, and prior defects must be explicit.
5. **Intent-overreach attack.** Harmful semantic drift is assumed deliberate without evidence. Strategic intent, negligence, emergence, and ordinary error are distinct findings.
6. **Behavior-gap attack.** Language changes but conduct does not, or conduct changes without ontology revision. The theory's causal pathway must be tested rather than presumed.
7. **Power-asymmetry attack.** Official records appear stable while affected people cannot contest categories. Contradiction permeability includes access and remedy.
8. **Cross-domain overreach.** A diagnostic from one institutional setting is applied to science, law, medicine, culture, or software without domain law and evidence.

### Claim and evidence ceiling

The paper offers a falsifiable diagnostic for consequential semantic change under strategic pressure. It does not determine political truth, legal corruption, scientific validity, motive, or institutional legitimacy by itself. Ontology change is neither presumptively corrupt nor presumptively benign.

Any concrete institutional claim requires exact records, authorized baselines, affected-party evidence, behavioral measures, alternative explanations, current domain sources, and qualified review. Internal corpus examples establish the analytic form only. No external research was added in this pass.

### Reader-facing consequence

The public question is whether the categories used to judge action were changed through an accountable, evidence-responsive process - or changed so that the same conduct could pass while objections became harder to express or hear.

## 17. Conclusion

Ontology laundering names a real candidate danger: a system can preserve procedure while changing the meanings by which procedure, evidence, authority, and failure are judged. The phrase is valuable only if it does not become its own semantic weapon.

The revised framework therefore places the burden on exact change custody, bounded correspondence, independent constraint, competing explanations, asymmetric-effect analysis, contradiction, appeal, and revision. It distinguishes legitimate correction from drift, negligence, strategic opacity, and deliberate deception. It permits inconclusive findings.

The central constitutional demand is not that meanings never change. It is that consequential semantic change remain visible enough to contest, evidence, compare, and revise before continuity of language is mistaken for continuity of obligation.
