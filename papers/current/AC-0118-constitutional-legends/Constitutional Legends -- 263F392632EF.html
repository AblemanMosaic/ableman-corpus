Constitutional Legends

Jurisdictional Projection Systems for Governed Semantic Participation

A symbol does not mean because it resembles. It means because a regime licensed participation.

Author
Adam Ableman
Constitutional Computing
Contact: ableman.research@gmail.com

Abstract

Modern semantic systems preserve:

  • syntax,
  • transport,
  • serialization,
  • and execution

far more rigorously than they preserve:

  • semantic continuity,
  • interpretive legitimacy,
  • projection authority,
  • and governed semantic participation.

Current architectures typically assume that meaning survives transformation implicitly through:

  • lexical stability,
  • embedding proximity,
  • operational usefulness,
  • or statistical coherence.

Constitutional Legends reject these assumptions.

This paper introduces the Constitutional Legend: a jurisdictional semantic projection system governing how meanings participate within declared interpretive regimes.

A Constitutional Legend is not merely:

  • a lookup table,
  • tokenizer,
  • ontology,
  • rendering layer,
  • or symbolic glossary.

It is:

  • an authority-bearing projection regime governing:

    • admissible semantic bindings,
    • render surfaces,
    • transport regimes,
    • continuity constraints,
    • admissible semantic participation,
    • and interpretive authority.

The paper formalizes:

  • canonical semantic identity layers,
  • semantic topology systems,
  • projection functions,
  • jurisdictional semantic views,
  • cross-language semantic continuity,
  • governed semantic transport,
  • and legend-relative projection continuity.

The paper further argues that semantic systems should separate:

  • semantic identity,
  • semantic topology,
  • projection jurisdiction,
  • transport,
  • and rendering

into independently governable layers.

The result is a semantic architecture in which:

  • symbols become governed projection surfaces,
  • projections become inspectable semantic operations,
  • semantic transport becomes auditable,
  • and continuity becomes jurisdiction-relative rather than implicitly assumed.

1. Introduction

Most semantic systems flatten:

  • meaning,
  • representation,
  • transport,
  • and interpretation

into a single operational layer.

Words become meanings. Tokens become concepts. Embeddings become semantic geometry. Renderings become semantic identity.

This flattening is computationally convenient.

It is architecturally unstable.

Modern systems routinely:

  • migrate tokenizers,
  • alter embeddings,
  • rewrite symbolic surfaces,
  • compress semantic context,
  • reinterpret terminology,
  • and mutate render regimes

while silently assuming continuity survives automatically.

Yet semantic continuity depends upon:

  • authority,
  • admissibility,
  • projection constraints,
  • and governed semantic participation.

Not:

  • lexical resemblance,
  • geometric proximity,
  • or operational usefulness alone.

The missing layer is the Constitutional Legend.

Not:

  • the traditional legend of maps or diagrams,

but:

  • a governed projection regime specifying:

    • what semantic bindings participate,
    • how meanings render,
    • what continuity guarantees survive transformation,
    • and what semantic operations remain admissible within the projection boundary.

This paper argues that semantic systems require explicit jurisdictional projection systems governing semantic participation itself.

Such systems are best understood as:

governed semantic participation architectures.

2. The Failure of Implicit Projection

Modern semantic systems continuously project semantic state across:

  • tokenization,
  • embedding layers,
  • retrieval systems,
  • summarization,
  • translation,
  • symbolic compression,
  • serialization,
  • and conversational memory reconstruction.

These systems often preserve:

  • coherence,
  • plausibility,
  • and operational utility,

while silently altering:

  • semantic participation admissibility,
  • interpretive authority,
  • continuity assumptions,
  • or admissible semantic scope.

Consider a model migration scenario.

A classification system originally trained under embedding regime ( E_1 ) classifies:

  • “constitutional continuity” inside a governance corpus as:
  • preservation of admissible semantic identity under governed transformation.

A later embedding regime ( E_2 ) shifts the neighborhood topology:

  • continuity becomes clustered nearer:

    • institutional persistence,
    • operational reliability,
    • or legal continuity.

The downstream system continues functioning:

  • outputs remain coherent,
  • retrieval still appears relevant,
  • and embeddings remain geometrically stable locally.

Yet semantic participation has changed silently.

The system lacks:

  • declared continuity constraints ( C ),
  • projection jurisdiction ( J ),
  • interpretive authority ( A ),
  • and admissibility governance.

This is projection failure.

Projection divergence may preserve:

  • coherence,
  • plausibility,
  • and operational usefulness,

while violating:

  • continuity guarantees,
  • interpretive legitimacy,
  • or admissible semantic participation.

A projection may therefore remain:

  • coherent,
  • useful,
  • or operational,

without remaining:

  • valid under the governing legend.

Modern systems expose:

  • syntax,
  • embeddings,
  • token boundaries,
  • and retrieval lineage.

They rarely expose:

  • who authorized semantic participation,
  • what continuity guarantees survive projection,
  • or what projection constraints govern transformation.

Constitutional Legends externalize these hidden projection surfaces.

3. The Constitutional Legend

A Constitutional Legend is:

a jurisdictional semantic projection system governing admissible semantic participation under a declared interpretive regime.

Admissible semantic participation means:

a semantic item may be selected, bound, rendered, transported, and interpreted under a legend ( L ) without violating continuity constraints ( C ).

Formally:

L = (J, B, R, T, C, A)

Where:

  • ( J ): jurisdiction Scope of admissible semantic space, including:

    • domains,
    • languages,
    • symbol systems,
    • and participation boundaries.

  • ( B ): admissible bindings Authorized mappings between:

    • CSIL identities,
    • lexical forms,
    • symbols,
    • and semantic surfaces.

  • ( R ): render surfaces Permitted representational forms, including:

    • textual,
    • symbolic,
    • graphical,
    • multimodal,
    • or machine-interpretable surfaces.

  • ( T ): transport regimes Encoding and serialization constraints governing:

    • transport codecs,
    • encoding behavior,
    • and transmission admissibility.

  • ( C ): continuity constraints Enforceable invariants governing:

    • identity continuity,
    • interpretive continuity,
    • and projection continuity.

  • ( A ): authority model The mechanism that certifies admissibility and continuity under the legend, including:

    • static standards authorities,
    • delegated policy engines,
    • governance systems,
    • or distributed consensus regimes.

Validation is conjunctive:

  • a projection ( P_L ) is admissible only if all components of ( L ) are satisfied simultaneously.

If any component of ( L ) fails, ( P_L ) is rejected rather than silently degraded or partially accepted.

The Constitutional Legend is not:

  • the semantic substrate itself, nor:
  • the semantic topology itself.

Instead:

  • it governs how semantic structures participate operationally within a projection regime.

4. Canonical Semantic Identity

Constitutional Legends operate over a canonical semantic identity substrate.

This paper refers to this substrate as: CSIL (Canonical Semantic Identity Layer).

CSIL provides:

  • stable semantic identity independent of rendering.

For example:

CSIL:2884

may refer canonically to:

light::illumination

independent of:

  • language,
  • symbol surface,
  • transport codec,
  • or rendering regime.

This separation is foundational.

Semantic identity must survive:

  • translation,
  • rendering,
  • encoding,
  • and serialization transformations.

Without stable identity, projection continuity collapses into:

  • surface-level approximation.

5. Semantic Topology

Canonical semantic identity alone produces:

  • lookup capability.

Semantic topology introduces:

  • neighborhood,
  • adjacency,
  • disambiguation,
  • and semantic traversal.

This paper refers to the semantic topology layer as: Semantic Topology Graph (STG).

An STG node minimally contains:

{
  csil_id,
  term,
  sense,
  definition,
  symbol,
  neighbors[],
  domain,
  layer
}

The symbol is not the semantic identity.

It is:

a render handle governed by ( L ).

STG transforms semantic identity space into:

  • navigable semantic topology.

This topology supports:

  • synonym traversal,
  • antonym traversal,
  • domain clustering,
  • grammatical disambiguation,
  • cross-language continuity,
  • and contextual sense selection.

STG defines:

  • possible semantic moves.

The legend ( L ) defines:

  • which moves are admissible.

6. Projection

A projection is a function:

P_L : (CSIL, STG, q) -> Surface

parameterized by:

  • legend ( L ),
  • and projection query ( q ).

The projection query may specify:

  • domain,
  • language,
  • symbol system,
  • transport regime,
  • continuity policy,
  • or render constraints.

Projection is not:

  • rendering alone.

Projection includes:

  • admissibility filtering,
  • topology traversal,
  • continuity enforcement,
  • transport encoding,
  • and render selection.

A valid projection must:

  • restrict to admissible jurisdiction ( J ),
  • select admissible bindings ( B ),
  • enforce continuity constraints ( C ),
  • choose admissible render surfaces ( R ),
  • apply transport rules ( T ),
  • and satisfy authority requirements ( A ).

Projection validity under ( L ) means:

the projected surface is admissible only if its jurisdiction, bindings, render surface, transport behavior, continuity guarantees, and authority requirements satisfy the governing legend simultaneously.

Plainly:

a projection is valid only when the output, its bindings, transport, continuity, and authority all satisfy the legend together.

Because ( L ) is explicit, projections become auditable.

One may verify whether a projected surface satisfies:

  • ( J ),
  • ( B ),
  • ( R ),
  • ( T ),
  • ( C ),
  • and ( A ).

Legend-relative projection continuity is defined as:

preservation of admissibility and continuity guarantees under projection transformations governed by ( L ).

7. Minimal Operational Example

Consider the lexical surface:

light

Define:

L_phys = (
  J = physics_domain,
  B = english_bindings,
  R = english_text_surface,
  T = Huffman_v3,
  C = strict_identity,
  A = governance_policy_v1
)

The system performs:

  1. semantic input:

    • “light”

  2. semantic resolution:

    • CSIL:2884

  3. topology traversal:

    • STG neighborhood favors:

      • illumination,
      • photon,
      • brightness

  4. jurisdiction filtering:

    • rejects:

      • CSIL:7712 (light::low_weight)

    • because:

      • jurisdiction ( J ) excludes non-physics senses

  5. continuity enforcement:

    • ( C = strict_identity )
    • prevents substitution with adjacent STG nodes

  6. surface selection:

    • English text surface under ( R )

  7. transport encoding:

    • Huffman_v3 under ( T )

  8. output:

    • “light”

Now define:

L_es = (
  J = general_domain,
  B = spanish_bindings,
  R = spanish_text_surface,
  T = utf8_transport,
  C = strict_identity,
  A = governance_policy_v1
)

The system performs:

  1. semantic input:

    • CSIL:2884

  2. topology traversal:

    • STG sense remains:

      • light::illumination

  3. continuity enforcement:

    • ( C = strict_identity )

    • prevents substitution with adjacent senses such as:

      • brightness,
      • lamp,
      • or visibility

  4. surface selection:

    • Spanish text surface under ( R )

  5. transport encoding:

    • utf8_transport under ( T )

  6. output:

    • “luz”

The semantic identity remains stable.

The projection changes.

8. Semantic Continuity Classes

The paper distinguishes three orthogonal but partially coupled continuity classes.

These are distinct dimensions, not nested levels, and may be checked independently.

  • Identity continuity Stable CSIL identity across projection.

  • Interpretive continuity Stable semantic sense under continuity constraints ( C ).

  • Projection continuity Stability of ( P_L ) outputs across transformation.

These continuity classes may diverge.

A system may preserve:

  • identity continuity, while:
  • violating projection continuity.

Or preserve:

  • lexical continuity, while:
  • violating interpretive continuity.

Continuity constraints ( C ) define:

  • invariants that must hold across projection transformations.

Violations render:

  • ( P_L ) invalid.

Examples include:

  • identity-preserving constraints,
  • sense-preserving constraints,
  • or monotonic constraints preventing semantic broadening.

9. Legends as Semantic Queries

A mature legend behaves operationally as:

a semantic query over semantic topology.

Example:

show:
- engineering domain
- English + symbolic render
- admissible governance terms
- Huffman transport v3
- strict continuity regime

The resulting projection becomes:

  • contextual,
  • authority-relative,
  • transport-aware,
  • and continuity-scoped.

Importantly: projection conflicts may emerge.

For example:

  • a requested render surface may lack admissible bindings,
  • a transport regime may violate continuity constraints ( C ),
  • or a requested language may fall outside jurisdiction ( J ).

Projection therefore becomes:

  • a governed semantic operation, not:
  • unconditional rendering.

11. Relationship to Existing Systems

Constitutional Legends overlap partially with:

  • ontologies,
  • tokenizers,
  • embeddings,
  • semantic graphs,
  • and type systems.

However: existing systems generally lack explicit projection jurisdiction.

  • RDF / OWL ontologies Preserve semantic structure but lack projection authority and continuity governance.

  • Embedding spaces Encode statistical similarity but lack stable semantic identity.

  • Tokenizers Govern segmentation but not semantic continuity constraints.

  • Type systems Govern structural validity but not semantic transport admissibility.

  • Lexicons and thesauri Provide lexical relationships but not topology-relative projection governance.

These systems encode:

  • structure,
  • similarity,
  • or representation.

The main systems contribution of Constitutional Legends is the integration of:

  • stable semantic identity substrates,
  • explicit projection jurisdiction,
  • enforceable continuity constraints,
  • and authority-bearing admissibility.

These systems do not govern whether a semantic projection is:

admissible under transformation.

12. Projection Divergence

Projection divergence occurs when:

  • surface coherence survives, while:
  • continuity constraints ( C ) fail beneath the projection layer.

Examples include:

  • tokenizer migration,
  • symbol reuse,
  • embedding reinterpretation,
  • translation drift,
  • ontology mutation,
  • or authority substitution.

Projection divergence preserves:

  • coherence,
  • plausibility,
  • and operational usefulness,

while violating:

  • admissible semantic continuity,
  • interpretive legitimacy,
  • or projection validity under ( L ).

Coherent outputs are not enough. Useful outputs are not enough. Only admissible outputs remain valid under the governing legend.

Constitutional Legends externalize:

  • continuity scope,
  • admissibility boundaries,
  • and interpretive authority

before divergence becomes operationally invisible.

15. Grammar, Inference, and Personality

Semantic topology alone remains:

  • navigable, but:
  • non-compositional.

Grammar introduces:

  • admissible semantic composition.

Inference introduces:

  • governed semantic traversal.

Personality introduces:

  • controlled stochastic traversal policies over admissible semantic topology.

Importantly: personality is not a property of:

  • the semantic substrate,
  • semantic identity,
  • or semantic topology itself.

It is:

  • a traversal policy operating within:

    • jurisdiction ( J ),
    • continuity constraints ( C ),
    • authority model ( A ),
    • and STG traversal boundaries.

Two systems may therefore share:

  • identical CSIL identity,
  • identical topology,
  • and identical continuity guarantees,

while producing:

  • stylistically distinct outputs.

Personality becomes:

a sampling distribution over admissible semantic moves.

It remains downstream of:

  • projection,
  • grammar,
  • and traversal policy,

not a new semantic primitive.

16. Implementation Sketch

A minimal projection pipeline may be expressed as:

function project(input, q, L):
    semantic_input = resolve_input(input, CSIL)
    candidates = lookup_candidates(semantic_input, STG)
    candidates = restrict_jurisdiction(candidates, L.J)
    candidates = select_admissible_bindings(candidates, L.B)
    traversed = traverse_stg(candidates, q)
    filtered = enforce_continuity(traversed, L.C)
    surface = choose_render_surface(filtered, L.R)
    encoded = apply_transport(surface, L.T)

    require satisfies_authority(encoded, L.A)

    return encoded

The operational stages are:

  • input resolution,
  • candidate generation,
  • jurisdiction filtering,
  • binding selection,
  • topology traversal,
  • continuity enforcement,
  • surface selection,
  • transport encoding,
  • and authority validation.

A corresponding validation procedure may be expressed as:

function validate_projection(P_L, L):
    require satisfies_jurisdiction(P_L, L.J)
    require satisfies_bindings(P_L, L.B)
    require satisfies_render_surface(P_L, L.R)
    require satisfies_transport(P_L, L.T)
    require satisfies_continuity(P_L, L.C)
    require satisfies_authority(P_L, L.A)

    return admissible

Projection validity under ( L ) therefore becomes:

  • inspectable,
  • testable,
  • and auditable.

17. Limitations

Several limitations remain.

Legend construction is nontrivial:

  • semantic topology design,
  • continuity governance,
  • and admissibility specification require substantial coordination effort.

Legend design may be underdetermined in some domains:

  • multiple plausible jurisdictions,
  • competing semantic partitions,
  • or unresolved sense boundaries may prevent a single obvious projection regime.

Legends may also fail to compose globally across domains:

  • independently valid projection regimes may remain incompatible without explicit mediation layers.

Authority disputes may remain unresolved:

  • different legends may authorize incompatible semantic projections.

Cross-legend interoperability may require:

  • explicit mediation,
  • continuity negotiation,
  • or jurisdictional translation layers.

Additionally:

  • semantic topology itself may evolve, requiring:
  • governed migration procedures,
  • continuity audits,
  • and explicit revalidation of admissibility constraints.

This reframes:

Modern semantic systems preserve:

  • syntax,
  • transport,
  • and execution

far more rigorously than they preserve:

  • projection legitimacy,
  • admissible semantic continuity,
  • and interpretive authority.

Constitutional Legends externalize:

  • projection jurisdiction,
  • admissible semantic participation,
  • continuity constraints,
  • transport legitimacy,
  • and interpretive governance

This architecture separates:

  • semantic identity,
  • semantic topology,
  • projection governance,
  • transport,
  • rendering,
  • grammar,
  • inference,
  • and personality

into independently governable layers.

The result is a semantic architecture in which:

  • semantic transport becomes auditable,

  • heterogeneous AI systems exchange governed continuity rather than merely exchanging tokens,

  • and model migrations become jurisdictional transitions requiring explicit revalidation of:

    • ( B ),
    • ( C ),
    • and ( A ) under successor legends ( L’ ).

The important question therefore ceases to be:

what does the symbol resemble?

The deeper question becomes:

under what jurisdiction was its participation licensed?

Reference-pass counterevidence and limit search

The pass applied the following pressure tests: Formal vocabularies and graph links make commitments explicit but cannot by themselves establish correct identity, shared interpretation, or lossless translation between contexts. These limits are not generic disclaimers: they identify the assumptions that a later empirical, formal, or implementation review must try to break.
