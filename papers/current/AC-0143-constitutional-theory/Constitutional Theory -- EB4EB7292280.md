## Abstract
Across physics, biology, cognition, economics, language, and institutions, explanation has traditionally proceeded by identifying entities and describing their interactions. Yet this approach repeatedly encounters the same boundary: entities alone cannot explain why certain transformations preserve identity while others dissolve it; why some futures remain available while others become impossible; or why systems exhibit continuity despite continual change.

This paper proposes **Constitutional Theory**, a general framework for understanding persistence, meaning, intelligence, coordination, and evolution in terms of **admissible continuation**. A constitution is defined not as a legal document, but as any structure that determines which continuations of a system remain lawful continuations of that system.

Constitutional Theory treats admissibility as prior to agency, persistence as prior to identity, and continuation as prior to description. Within this framework, meaning becomes the alteration of future possibility; intelligence becomes the preservation of viable continuation; hierarchy becomes the compression of contradiction; markets become mechanisms for constitutional coordination; and physical persistence becomes a special case of constitutional binding.

The theory does not compete with existing scientific descriptions. Rather, it proposes a deeper explanatory layer concerned not with what systems do, but with what they are permitted to become.

---

## 1. The Problem of Persistence

Every explanatory framework eventually encounters a question it cannot answer using its own primitives.

Physics explains motion but struggles to explain why lawful structure persists.

Biology explains adaptation but struggles to explain why organisms remain identifiable across continual replacement.

Economics explains exchange but struggles to explain why markets maintain coherence.

Cognitive science explains computation but struggles to explain meaning.

Political theory explains institutions but struggles to explain legitimacy.

These difficulties share a common form.

They concern neither behavior nor structure alone.

They concern persistence.

Why does a thing remain itself while changing?

Why do some transformations preserve identity while others destroy it?

Why are some futures available and others refused?

The present work proposes that these questions belong to a common category.

---

## 2. The Constitutional Turn

Constitutional Theory begins with a shift in explanatory focus.

Traditional explanations ask:

> What is a thing?

Constitutional explanations ask:

> What must remain true for this thing to continue as itself?

This shift replaces ontology centered on entities with ontology centered on admissibility.

The primary object of inquiry becomes neither substance nor process.

It becomes continuation.

---

## 3. Fundamental Definitions

### 3.1 Continuation

A continuation is any transformation connecting one state of a system to another.

Not all continuations are equivalent.

Some preserve the system.

Some alter it.

Some terminate it.

The distinction between these possibilities cannot be derived solely from state descriptions.

It depends upon constitutional constraints.

---

### 3.2 Constitution

A constitution is the structure that determines which continuations remain admissible.

A constitution need not be written, explicit, formalized, or conscious.

A constitution may exist within:

* a physical system,
* an organism,
* a language,
* a market,
* an institution,
* a mind,
* a civilization.

The constitution governs lawful transformation.

---

### 3.3 Admissibility

A continuation is admissible when it preserves the constitutional conditions required for persistence.

Admissibility is not identical to probability.

Rare events may be admissible.

Common events may be inadmissible.

Admissibility concerns possibility under constitutional constraint.

---

### 3.4 Refusal

Refusal occurs when a continuation violates constitutional conditions.

Refusal may appear as:

* physical resistance,
* informational inconsistency,
* institutional collapse,
* cognitive contradiction,
* economic insolvency,
* biological death.

Refusal is the negative expression of constitution.

A constitution is known by what it prevents.

---

## 4. Constitutional Invariants

For Constitutional Theory to remain meaningful across domains, certain invariants must hold.

### 4.1 Identity Is Not Primitive

Identity emerges from preserved continuation.

It does not precede it.

---

### 4.2 Persistence Requires Constraint

Unconstrained possibility cannot generate stable systems.

Persistence requires refusal.

---

### 4.3 Meaning Alters Future Space

A distinction possesses meaning only insofar as it changes admissible continuation.

Meaning is therefore constitutional.

---

### 4.4 Intelligence Preserves Viable Continuation

Intelligence is not fundamentally computation.

It is the capacity to preserve or enlarge viable future space.

---

### 4.5 Coordination Is Constitutional

Coordination emerges whenever multiple agents participate within overlapping admissibility structures.

---

### 4.6 Evolution Is Constitutional Self-Modification

Evolution modifies constitutions while preserving enough continuity to remain identifiable.

---

## 5. Constitutional Domains

The framework applies across multiple levels of analysis.

---

### 5.1 Constitutional Meaning

Meaning is not reference.

Meaning is constitutional consequence.

A distinction matters when it changes the space of future possibilities.

Language functions by altering admissibility.

Communication is the transfer of constitutional constraint.

---

### 5.2 Constitutional Intelligence

Intelligence is the maintenance of viable continuation.

A system demonstrates intelligence when it preserves future possibility under constraint.

Optimization is secondary.

Continuation is primary.

---

### 5.3 Constitutional Markets

Markets coordinate distributed agents by transforming local constraints into collective admissibility structures.

Prices are constitutional signals.

Contracts are constitutional commitments.

Debt represents deferred contradiction.

Bankruptcy represents constitutional refusal.

---

### 5.4 Constitutional Hierarchy

Hierarchy is not fundamental.

Hierarchy emerges when contradictions exceed the capacity of decentralized resolution.

Coordinators compress contradiction.

The necessity of hierarchy is therefore contingent rather than absolute.

---

### 5.5 Constitutional Evolution

Evolution is not merely selection.

Evolution is constitutional adaptation.

Successful evolution preserves continuity while altering admissibility.

Failure occurs when constitutional modification exceeds identity preservation.

---

### 5.6 Constitutional Physics

At the physical level, inertia may be interpreted as constitutional resistance to unauthorized transformation.

Persistence of lawful structure becomes a special case of constitutional binding.

Physical law appears not merely as dynamics but as admissibility itself.

---

## 6. Relationship to Complexity

Constitutional Theory differs from descriptive complexity.

Kolmogorov complexity asks:

> What is the shortest description capable of generating a system?

Constitutional Theory asks:

> What constraints determine which generated futures remain admissible?

Generation and continuation are distinct questions.

A universe may possess minimal generative complexity while exhibiting immense constitutional complexity.

The shortest program does not necessarily imply the simplest future.

---

## 7. Relationship to Existing Frameworks

### Physics

Physics describes lawful transformation.

Constitutional Theory addresses lawful admissibility.

---

### Information Theory

Information theory measures distinctions.

Constitutional Theory measures the significance of distinctions for continuation.

---

### Systems Theory

Systems theory studies interactions.

Constitutional Theory studies the constraints that make interaction coherent.

---

### Evolutionary Theory

Evolutionary theory explains adaptation.

Constitutional Theory explains the preservation conditions under which adaptation remains possible.

---

### Political Constitutionalism

Political constitutions govern societies.

Constitutional Theory generalizes the concept to any persistence-capable system.

---

## 8. An Explicit Impossibility

The framework implies a fundamental impossibility.

**No system may preserve identity across transformations that violate the constitution defining that identity.**

Equivalently:

Identity cannot survive the destruction of the conditions that make identity possible.

This impossibility appears trivial when stated abstractly.

Yet much of theoretical confusion arises from attempting to preserve entities while discarding the constraints that constitute them.

Constitutional Theory rejects such continuations as inadmissible.

---

## 9. Scope and Limits

This framework is not a predictive theory.

It supplies no equations of motion.

It introduces no new forces.

It proposes no privileged mechanism.

Its purpose is ontological.

It identifies a common explanatory substrate beneath persistence, meaning, intelligence, coordination, and evolution.

Whether future formalization renders the framework mathematically precise remains an open question.

---

## Conclusion

The deepest question facing explanation is not what exists.

It is what persists.

Across every domain, systems survive by navigating a landscape of permissible transformation.

Constitutions determine that landscape.

Meaning changes it.

Intelligence preserves it.

Evolution modifies it.

Hierarchy compresses its contradictions.

Markets coordinate within it.

Physics projects it into lawful structure.

Constitutional Theory therefore proposes a simple inversion:

The future is not built from things.

Things are built from the continuations that survive.

Persistence precedes identity.

Admissibility precedes agency.

Constitution precedes description.
