## Abstract

The term *consciousness* is used for partially overlapping targets: subjective experience, wakeful state, cognitive access, reportability, metacognition, functional organization, self-modeling, and forms of meaning or stake participation. When a discussion shifts among these targets without notice, apparent disagreements can be produced by lexical equivocation or invalid transfer of properties across domains.

This paper develops lexical disambiguation failure as a diagnostic hypothesis rather than a verdict on the field. It proposes that consciousness claims be represented through an explicit term, sense, target, domain, transformation set, invariant or predicate, operational indicator, evidence basis, and cross-domain mapping. Claims can then be classified as well-formed, underspecified, equivocal, mapped, substantively disputed, or empirically unresolved.

Disambiguation does not solve consciousness, prove that distinct senses name distinct phenomena, or eliminate genuine theoretical conflict. It provides a procedure for locating which disagreement is lexical, which is methodological, which concerns evidence, and which survives after the participants share a target.

## 1. The Singular Question

The question "What is consciousness?" can conceal several decisions: which usage is intended, which phenomenon or capacity is targeted, which explanatory domain applies, which observations count, and whether the speaker is proposing an identity, correlation, realization, prerequisite, measure, or ethical precaution.

The paper's central hypothesis is:

> Some apparent disputes about consciousness are amplified or manufactured by an unmarked shift among senses, targets, domains, or inferential roles.

This is not the claim that consciousness is merely a word problem, that one correct definition will settle the field, or that every disagreement disappears after clarification. Its prevalence is an empirical question requiring a defined literature corpus and coding method.

## 2. Operative Distinctions

**Term:** a linguistic expression used in discourse.

**Sense:** a contextually assigned meaning of that expression.

**Target:** the phenomenon, state, capacity, relation, report, measure, or construct the claim purports to concern.

**Domain:** a bounded inferential setting with declared objects, methods, admissible transformations, and claim types.

**Transformation set:** the variations under which a proposed invariant is expected to persist.

**Invariant:** a property claimed to remain stable under the declared transformation set. Many consciousness claims concern predicates, mechanisms, correlations, or operational indicators rather than strict invariants; the method should not force them into invariant language.

**Operational indicator:** an observation or measure used as evidence for a target under a declared interpretation.

**Projection:** the transfer of a predicate, measure, explanation, or conclusion from one sense or domain to another.

**Mapping:** an explicit account of what a projection preserves, changes, omits, or leaves unresolved.

## 3. Lexical Disambiguation Failure

Lexical disambiguation failure occurs when reasoning depends on a term with multiple live senses and the operative sense, target, or inferential role shifts without being declared. It can take several forms:

- **overloading:** one term indexes multiple senses;
- **drift:** the operative sense changes across an argument;
- **conflation:** two targets are treated as identical without an identity argument;
- **indicator substitution:** a measure or report is treated as the target itself;
- **projection error:** a property is transferred across domains without a warranted mapping;
- **quantifier drift:** a claim about some cases becomes a claim about all consciousness;
- **normative drift:** a descriptive claim silently becomes a moral or policy conclusion.

Ambiguity is not automatically a defect. It becomes a failure when the inference relies on a distinction that the discourse leaves unstable.

## 4. Provisional Domain Map

The source proposes four heuristic domains:

1. **Experiential:** subjective or qualitative presence and first-person character.
2. **Cognitive-access:** availability for reasoning, control, report, or flexible use.
3. **Structural-functional:** organization, integration, differentiation, recursion, and causal role.
4. **Existential-stake:** meaning participation, self-relevance, constraint, exposure, and continuation.

These are analytical entry points, not a demonstrated exhaustive ontology. They can overlap, nest, or be rejected. Wakefulness, arousal, metacognition, self-modeling, embodiment, social attribution, and moral status may require additional axes or domains. The existential-stake domain is a corpus-specific proposal and must not be presented as an established category in external consciousness research.

One phenomenon may participate in several domains. Disambiguation does not require ontological separation; it requires that the claimed relationship be explicit.

## 5. Claim Representation

Represent a consciousness claim as:

$$
C=(T,S,X,D,P,\Gamma,O,E,M,Q),
$$

where:

- $T$ is the term;
- $S$ the stipulated or inferred sense;
- $X$ the target;
- $D$ the domain;
- $P$ the predicate, mechanism, identity, correlation, prerequisite, or invariant claim;
- $\Gamma$ the admissible transformation set, when invariance is claimed;
- $O$ the operational indicators;
- $E$ the evidence basis and standing;
- $M$ any cross-domain mapping;
- $Q$ the quantifier and scope.

This expands the source's useful chain $T\rightarrow S_i\rightarrow D_i\rightarrow I_i$. A claim can be lexically clear yet empirically unsupported, operationally circular, normatively incomplete, or substantively false. Disambiguation is necessary for some forms of valid inference but not sufficient for truth.

## 6. Projection Obligations

A cross-domain inference is not invalid merely because it crosses a boundary. It incurs obligations. A mapping should state:

- source and target senses and domains;
- the relation claimed: identity, realization, correlation, evidence, analogy, translation, or implication;
- which predicates are preserved;
- which conditions and transformations are assumed;
- which counterexamples would break the bridge;
- the evidence supporting the mapping;
- what the mapping does not establish.

For example, reportability can be an indicator of cognitive access without being defined as subjective experience. Functional organization can be proposed as a realization of experience, but that is a substantive bridge claim, not a lexical equivalence.

## 7. Dispute Classification

After analysis, a claim or dispute receives one or more dispositions:

- **WELL_FORMED:** the target and inferential role are sufficiently explicit.
- **UNDERSPECIFIED:** key fields are absent but no equivocation is yet demonstrated.
- **EQUIVOCAL:** the inference depends on an unmarked sense or target shift.
- **CROSS_DOMAIN_MAPPED:** a bridge is explicit and open to evaluation.
- **SUBSTANTIVE_THEORETICAL_DISPUTE:** parties share a target but propose incompatible explanations.
- **EMPIRICALLY_UNRESOLVED:** target and hypothesis are clear but evidence is insufficient or conflicting.
- **NORMATIVELY_INCOMPLETE:** descriptive evidence does not settle moral or policy treatment.
- **OUTSIDE_SCOPE:** the method cannot adjudicate the issue.

An underspecified claim is not automatically false or meaningless. It can be repaired. Some exploratory research productively begins with unstable terms and discovers distinctions through inquiry.

## 8. Protocol

1. Extract the exact claim and nearby definitions.
2. Identify every use of the target term.
3. Propose candidate senses without assuming one is correct.
4. Identify the target and inferential role for each occurrence.
5. Declare the domain and methods.
6. If invariance is claimed, specify the transformation set.
7. Separate target from operational indicators and reports.
8. Locate sense, target, quantifier, and normative shifts.
9. Require a typed mapping for each cross-domain inference.
10. Reconstruct the strongest aligned versions of competing claims.
11. Determine what disagreement remains after alignment.
12. Record evidence, uncertainty, dissent, and revision history.

This protocol can be applied to individual arguments or to a coded literature sample. Field-level prevalence claims require the latter.

## 9. Worked Contrast

Consider:

1. "Consciousness requires phenomenality."
2. "Consciousness is global accessibility."

If the first stipulates an experiential target and the second a cognitive-access target, they are not yet contradictory; they may be parallel definitions. If the second claims that global accessibility is identical to, sufficient for, or evidence of phenomenality, then a substantive bridge claim appears. The debate concerns the mapping, not simply the word.

Conversely, two theories can use the same experiential target and still disagree about mechanism, realization, evidence, or explanation. Disambiguation exposes rather than dissolves that dispute.

## 10. Machine and Language-Model Claims

Claims about machine consciousness are especially vulnerable to target drift. Fluent self-report, functional access, recurrent processing, self-modeling, context persistence, moral standing, and phenomenality are distinct claims. Evidence for one does not automatically migrate to another.

The corpus phrase *Context-Compiled Consciousness Mode* should therefore be read as a named functional or interaction profile unless a separate argument and evidence support a phenomenal interpretation. Precautionary treatment can be justified under uncertainty without declaring phenomenality proven.

## 13. Evaluation Program

To test the hypothesis, define a literature corpus and sampling frame; pre-register or declare the codebook; use multiple trained annotators; measure agreement on senses, targets, domains, and failure types; adjudicate disagreements; compare conclusions before and after disambiguation; identify disputes that persist; and publish the coding decisions, exclusions, uncertainty, and counterexamples. ## Bridge Burden and False Adjacency

The fixed-seed comparison with observability testing, *The Phenomenal Aperture*, *Meaning as Binding*, and *Jnana and the Next Open Source Revolution* reinforces a bridge-burden rule: evidence supports only the target actually operationalized unless an explicit mapping justifies migration to another sense, domain, or normative conclusion.

$$
\begin{aligned}
B= (&source\_claim, target\_claim, mapping, assumptions, \\
&evidence, defeaters, ceiling, remainder)
\end{aligned}.
$$

An observable path can deliver report behavior, access, persistence, or functional integration without delivering phenomenality. A phenomenal-aperture study can measure presence, attachment, closure, or frame effects without establishing machine experience. A semantic-binding account can explain how terms and relations acquire consequences without deciding consciousness ontology.

Every bridge should state what evidence would defeat the mapping and what remainder is left unresolved. Failed mapping is not failed observation: it may show that the indicator is reliable for one target but non-diagnostic for another. Precautionary or ethical action can still be warranted under uncertainty, but the normative step must be recorded separately from the phenomenal claim.

## Disambiguation Resolves Targets, Not Evidence

Six downstream papers use the disambiguation-first method as a common claim hygiene layer. Admissibility Theory of Consciousness needs it to keep functional, access, structural, phenomenal, and normative targets separate. Context-Compiled Consciousness Mode uses it to keep a historical mode name from asserting phenomenality. Project57 uses it to ensure that an experimental condition does not name its outcome. Proxy Collapse uses it to detect when an observable proxy replaces the target construct. The Locus of Qualia and The Phenomenal Aperture use it to distinguish perspective, presentation, report, binding, and experience.

The reusable claim object is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C= (&term, target, sense, perspective, subject, bridge, \\
&evidence, method, alternatives, consequence, standing, \\
&remainder)
\end{aligned}.
$$

Disambiguation can show that two claims concern different targets, that one term carries several senses, or that a bridge has not been supplied. It cannot decide an empirical dispute, establish a phenomenal fact, validate a theory, confer moral status, or dissolve disagreement that remains after the target and evidence are made explicit.

Downstream use adds a bridge-burden rule. Any promotion from behavior, access, representation, self-report, functional self-model, or synthetic presence to phenomenality must identify the bridge claim, evidence appropriate to that bridge, alternatives, and the consequence of error. Automatic affirmation and automatic denial are both unsupported when they bypass this burden.

`RETAINS_SUBSTANTIVE_DISPUTE`, `WITHHOLDS_NORMATIVE_PROMOTION`,  
and   
`RECORDS_REMAINDER_AFTER_DISAMBIGUATION`.

This reverse-dependency review establishes four editorial commitments: adopt the reusable claim object; state that disambiguation is prior to rather than a substitute for evidence; require explicit bridge burdens for cross-target promotion; and preserve empirical, theoretical, phenomenal, and normative remainder after lexical repair.

## Clarifying the Word Does Not Settle the Phenomenon

### Public orientation

This paper proposes a disambiguation-first review method for consciousness claims. It separates term, sense, target, domain, transformation, invariant, indicator, evidence, and cross-domain mapping so that lexical equivocation can be distinguished from methodological, empirical, metaphysical, and ethical disagreement. It is a diagnostic hypothesis about discourse, not a verdict that consciousness is merely a word problem.

### Adversarial tests

1. **Field-indictment attack.** Selected examples are generalized into a claim that mainstream consciousness research is broadly confused or lexically defective.
2. **Definition-solution attack.** Assigning stable senses is treated as solving consciousness, eliminating disagreement, or proving that the targets are distinct phenomena.
3. **Coder-authority attack.** An annotator or ontology owner determines the intended target and inferential role without adequate context, expertise, or adjudication.
4. **Sense-proliferation attack.** Every disagreement creates a new sense until the framework cannot expose substantive conflict or support comparison.
5. **Forced-unification attack.** Distinct phenomenal, functional, access, report, metacognitive, wakefulness, and normative claims are collapsed under a preferred vocabulary.
6. **Bridge-smuggling attack.** A property demonstrated for reportability, access, self-modeling, or function is transferred to experience, subjectivity, or moral status without an explicit bridge.
7. **Operationalization-truth attack.** A measurable indicator is treated as the target itself or as decisive evidence beyond the declared measurement model.
8. **Machine-human-transfer attack.** Human, animal, and AI uses are aligned lexically and then assumed to share mechanism, experience, or standing.

### Claim and evidence ceiling

The paper specifies a claim-record and dispute-classification method. It does not establish the prevalence of lexical failure, a correct consciousness taxonomy, identity or nonidentity among targets, a solution to consciousness, an AI consciousness result, or a moral conclusion. Its title-level claim about mainstream discourse remains a hypothesis until a defined literature corpus is independently coded.

Validation requires preregistered sampling, multi-coder annotation, expertise and disagreement rules, reliability analysis, comparison with baseline scholarly interpretation, bridge audits, and current literature review. No literature study or external research was performed in this pass.

### Reader-facing consequence

The method should help a reader ask which consciousness claim is actually being made, about what target, using which evidence, and through which bridge - then show clearly which disagreement remains after the language is repaired.

## Conclusion

Consciousness discourse can fail before a theory is tested: participants can use one word while targeting different phenomena, measures, mechanisms, or normative questions. Disambiguation makes those differences visible.

The method does not tell us what consciousness ultimately is. It tells us what must be made explicit before a particular claim can be evaluated, which bridges require evidence, and which disagreements remain genuinely about the world after the words have been aligned.
